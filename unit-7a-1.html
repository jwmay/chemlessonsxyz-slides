KINETIC-MOLECULAR THEORY
A Model for Gases
In 1860, chemists proposed a model to explain the properties of gases.
The kinetic-molecular theory (KMT) describes the behavior of matter in terms of particles in motion.
The model makes assumptions about the size, motion, and energy of gas particles.
💨
KINETIC = MOTION
particles are always moving
KINETIC-MOLECULAR THEORY

1. KMT — Particle Size

Gases consist of small particles that are separated from one another by empty space.
The volume of the particles is small compared with the volume of the empty space.
Because gas particles are far apart, they experience no significant attractive or repulsive forces.
KINETIC-MOLECULAR THEORY

2. KMT — Particle Motion

Gas particles are in constant, random motion.
Particles move in a straight line until they collide with other particles or the walls of their container.
Collisions between gas particles are elastic.
KINETIC-MOLECULAR THEORY

Elastic vs. Inelastic Collisions

Gas particles exert a pressure but don’t lose any energy during their collisions.
Elastic collisions ✅
An elastic collision is one in which no kinetic energy is lost. Same speed in, same speed out.
Example: gas particles
Inelastic collisions
Some kinetic energy is lost — to heat, sound, or a change in shape. The particles slow down.
Example: a ball of clay hitting the floor
KINETIC-MOLECULAR THEORY

3. KMT — Particle Energy

Mass and velocity are the two factors that determine the kinetic energy of a particle.
KINETIC ENERGY
KE = ½mv2
KEkinetic energy — the energy of motion
mmass of the particle
vvelocity (speed) of the particle
🌡️ Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample of matter.

THE BEHAVIOR OF GASES

1. Gases Have Low Density & Mass

Gases seem weightless, but they are classified as matter — therefore, they must have mass.
The density of a gas is much less than the density of a liquid or solid. That’s why we can walk through a room without worrying about air resistance.
The mass is really only noticeable if we have a collection of gas in a container.
GASES HAVE MASS 🏀

Inflated

THE BEHAVIOR OF GASES

2. Gases Are Compressible

Gas particles have a high velocity, relative to their low masses.
This high velocity and low mass means the particles are spread far apart, with empty space in between particles.
Therefore, if you squeeze on a gas, its volume can be reduced considerably.
THE BEHAVIOR OF GASES
3. Gases Fill Their Containers
Gases expand until they take up as much room as they possibly can.
Because gases are in constant motion, they spread out to fill their container.
This is why there is nowhere around you without air.
📦
NO EMPTY CORNERS
gas spreads everywhere
THE BEHAVIOR OF GASES

4. Gases Diffuse

Gases can flow easily past each other.
The random motion of the gas particles causes the gases to mix until they are evenly distributed.

VALVE OPENED

The movement of one substance through another is called diffusion.
THE BEHAVIOR OF GASES

Rate of Diffusion: Graham’s Law

The rate of diffusion depends mainly on the mass of the particles involved. Lighter particles diffuse more rapidly than heavier particles.
GRAHAM’S LAW

5. Effusion of Gases

Effusion is the escape of a gas through a tiny opening.
There is an inverse relationship between effusion rates and the molar mass of the gas.

🎈

Air balloon · ~29 g/mol

6. Gases Exert Pressure

Gas particles exert pressure by colliding with objects in their path.
The sum of all of the collisions makes up the pressure the gas exerts.
Pressure is the force per unit area.

AIR PRESSURE
Air Pressure
Earth is surrounded by an atmosphere made up of gases.
As the particles in the air move in every direction, they collide with the surface of the Earth and other objects.
That pressure is called atmospheric pressure, or air pressure.
🌍
AN OCEAN OF AIR
pushing on everything
AIR PRESSURE

Air Pressure Varies with Altitude

The lower the altitude, the longer and heavier the column of air pushing down on Earth’s surface…
…and the greater the air pressure. ⛰️ Higher up = less air above you = lower pressure.

AIR PRESSURE

Measuring Air Pressure: The Barometer

A barometer is an instrument used to measure atmospheric pressure.
The barometer was invented by Italian physicist Evangelista Torricelli, who was the first to show that the atmosphere exerted pressure.
Air pushing down on the dish holds up a column of mercury about 760 mm tall at sea level.

AIR PRESSURE

Measuring Gas Pressure: The Manometer

A manometer is an instrument used to measure gas pressure in a closed container.
A flask is connected to a U-tube that contains mercury.
The difference in the height of the mercury is used to calculate the pressure of the gas in the flask.

FORCES OF ATTRACTION

Three Types of Intermolecular Forces

From weakest to strongest:
London dispersion
Weakest
Temporary dipoles in nonpolar molecules.
Found in every molecule — the only IMF in nonpolar ones.
e.g. CH4, O2
Dipole-dipole
Stronger
Permanent attractions between polar molecules.
The δ+ end of one molecule pulls on the δ− end of the next.
e.g. HCl
Hydrogen bonds
Strongest
H bonded to O, N or F.
A special, extra-strong kind of dipole-dipole attraction.
e.g. H2O, NH3
FORCES OF ATTRACTION

London Dispersion Forces (LDFs)

LDFs are weak forces that result from temporary shifts in the density of electrons in the electron cloud of nonpolar molecules.
They arise from temporary dipoles between oppositely charged regions of the molecules.
FORCES OF ATTRACTION

Dipole-Dipole Forces

Dipole-dipole forces are permanent attractions between oppositely charged regions of polar molecules.
Neighboring polar molecules orient themselves so that oppositely charged regions align.
FORCES OF ATTRACTION

Hydrogen Bonds

A hydrogen bond is a special type of dipole-dipole attraction between molecules containing a hydrogen atom bonded to a small, highly electronegative atom (O, N, F).
Occurs only in compounds containing O—H, N—H, and F—H bonds.
The strongest intermolecular force. 💪
