LIQUIDS & SOLIDS
Liquids and Solids
Just as with gases, the properties of liquids and solids can be explained with the kinetic-molecular theory (KMT).
Liquids and solids are more dense than gases — this is why they are called the condensed states of matter.
Their particles are still in constant motion, but they aren’t independent of each other like a gas.

LIQUIDS

Fluidity

Fluidity is the ability to flow and diffuse. Both liquids and gases are fluids.
Gases 💨
Most fluid
Particles are far apart, with almost no attractions to slow them down.
Liquids 💧
Less fluid
Intermolecular attractions interfere with the flow.
Example: a drop of food coloring slowly flows and diffuses through a glass of water. 🧪
LIQUIDS

Viscosity 🍯

Viscosity is a measure of the resistance of a liquid to flow. Harder to pour = higher viscosity.
Stronger IMFs
🧲
⬆ viscosity
Particles are attracted to one another, so they resist sliding past each other.
Larger molecules
⛓️
⬆ viscosity
Viscosity increases with the size of the molecule — larger molecules move slower.
Higher temperature
🔥
⬇ viscosity
Added energy lets molecules overcome intermolecular forces and flow more freely.

LIQUIDS

Surface Tension

Surface tension is a force that pulls adjacent parts of a liquid’s surface together, decreasing the surface area to the smallest possible size.
A measure of the inward pull by particles in the interior.
The molecules at the surface of a liquid cling to each other.
LIQUIDS

Surface Tension & Water

The stronger the forces of attraction between the molecules, the stronger the surface tension.
Water: high surface tension
💧
Water has a relatively high surface tension because of its hydrogen bonding.
Beads & water striders
🕷️
Surface tension makes water bead up into spheres that reduce surface area — and lets a spider stand on a pond.

Surfactants
The high surface tension of water makes it difficult for water alone to remove dirt from skin and clothing.
Compounds that lower the surface tension of water are called surfactants.
Soaps and detergents decrease the surface tension of water by disrupting the hydrogen bonds between water molecules.

LIQUIDS

Capillary Action 🧻

Capillary action is the attraction of the surface of a liquid to the surface of a solid.
Cohesive forces
Intermolecular forces between the liquid molecules.
liquid ↔ liquid
Adhesive forces
Forces between the liquid and the solid (its container).
liquid ↔ solid
Two different types of forces are responsible for this property: cohesive and adhesive.
LIQUIDS

Concave vs. Convex Meniscus

A concave meniscus forms when the adhesive forces between the liquid and its container are stronger than the cohesive forces between the liquid molecules. 💧
A convex meniscus forms when the cohesive forces between the liquid molecules are greater than the adhesive forces between the liquid and its container.
Reading a graduated cylinder? Read a concave meniscus at the bottom of the curve.

SOLIDS

Solids

Solids contain particles with strong attractive intermolecular forces.
Particles in a solid vibrate in a fixed position.
The particles are tightly packed together, which results in a high density. Most solids are more dense than liquids.
Exception: ice floats in water. 🧊
SOLIDS

Crystalline Solids

A crystalline solid is a solid whose atoms, ions, or molecules are arranged in an orderly, geometric structure.
The locations of particles can be represented as points on a framework called a crystal lattice.
A unit cell is the smallest arrangement of atoms in a crystal lattice that has the same symmetry as the whole crystal.

SOLIDS

Atomic & Molecular Solids

1 · Atomic solids
Atoms at lattice points. The only atomic solids are the noble gases. Properties reflect the weak dispersion forces between atoms.
Characteristics: soft; very low melting points; poor conductivity
Examples: solid argon, solid neon
2 · Molecular solids
Molecules at lattice points, held together by IMFs.
Characteristics: fairly soft; low melting points; poor conductivity
Examples: H2O, CO2, C12H22O11 (sugar)
SOLIDS

Covalent Network & Ionic Solids

3 · Covalent network solids
Atoms connected by covalent bonds into one giant network.
Characteristics: very hard; very high melting points; often poor conductivity
Examples: diamond (C), quartz (SiO2)
4 · Ionic solids
Ions held together by ionic bonds.
Characteristics: hard and brittle; high melting points; poor conductivity
Examples: NaCl, KBr, CaCO3
SOLIDS

5 · Metallic Solids

Lattice points occupied by positive metal ions surrounded by a sea of mobile electrons.
Characteristics: soft to hard; low to very high melting points; malleable and ductile; excellent conductivity.
Examples: gold, silver 🥇

Phase Changes

Most substances can exist in three states depending on the temperature and pressure.
Each phase change is accompanied by a change in energy.
Magenta = energy absorbed · blue = energy released

PHASE DIAGRAMS

Phase Diagrams

A phase diagram is a graph of pressure versus temperature.
Shows in which phase a substance exists under different conditions of temperature and pressure.
PHASE DIAGRAMS

Triple Point & Critical Point

Three lines are the boundaries between the three phases. They separate the graph into sections: liquid, solid and gas.
The triple point: the temperature and pressure where the lines intersect — all three phases coexist.
The critical point: above this temperature and pressure the substance cannot exist as a liquid. Water: 374°C — no amount of pressure will liquefy it.
PHASE DIAGRAMS

Phase Diagrams: Water vs. Carbon Dioxide

Water, H2O 💧
At 1 atm: melts at 0°C, boils at 100°C. Critical point 374°C, 218 atm.
Carbon dioxide, CO2 🧊
Triple point at 5.11 atm, so at 1 atm it sublimes at −78.5°C — dry ice never melts. Critical pt 31.1°C, 73 atm.
