MEASURING GASES

Amount of Gas (n)

The quantity of gas in a sample is expressed in moles. Remember: 1 mole = 6.02 × 1023 particles.
MOLES FROM MASS
n = mass (g)molar mass (g/mol)
nnumber of moles of gas
gmass of the gas sample
g/molmolar mass, from the periodic table
Example: 16.0 g O2 ÷ 32.0 g/mol = 0.500 mol O2
MEASURING GASES
Volume (V)
A gas uniformly fills any container in which it is placed. Thus the volume of a gas is simply the volume of its container.
The metric unit of volume, the liter (L), is often used. There are 1000 mL in 1 L.
A 2-liter bottle full of air holds 2 L of gas. 🥤
📦
V = CONTAINER
gas fills it completely
MEASURING GASES

Temperature (T) 🌡️

Measured with a thermometer in Celsius — but all gas calculations must be done in Kelvin.
CELSIUS KELVIN
K = °C + 273
25°C + 273 = 298 K
KELVIN CELSIUS
°C = K − 273
373 K − 273 = 100°C
Kelvin starts at absolute zero (0 K) — never negative, so gas-law ratios work.

THE GAS LAWS
The Gas Laws
Studies of the behavior of gases played a major role in the development of physical sciences in the 17th and 18th centuries.
Observations have become mathematical laws which we can use to predict quantitative outcomes.
The equations that express the relationships among T, P, V, and n are known as the gas laws.
📐
OBSERVE PREDICT
math from experiments
BOYLE’S LAW

Boyle’s Law: Pressure & Volume

Robert Boyle was among the first to note the inverse relationship between pressure and volume of a gas.
He measured the volume of air at different pressures and observed a pattern of behavior.
As the pressure on a gas increases, the volume of the gas decreases. (T and n constant)

BOYLE’S LAW

Boyle’s Mathematical Law

Pressure × volume stays constant when temperature and amount of gas don’t change.
BOYLE’S LAW
P1V1 = P2V2
P1, V1starting pressure and volume
P2, V2new pressure and volume
T, nheld constant
Units must match on both sides — any pressure unit, any volume unit.
BOYLE’S LAW

Example: Boyle’s Law

A gas has a volume of 3.0 L at 2 atm. What will its volume be at 4 atm?
STEP 1 · LIST THE VARIABLES
P1 = 2 atm · V1 = 3.0 L · P2 = 4 atm · V2 = ?
STEP 2 · CHOOSE THE LAW
Boyle’s law: P1V1 = P2V2
STEP 3 · PLUG IN & CALCULATE
V2 = P1V1P2 = (2 atm)(3.0 L)4 atm = 1.5 L
CHARLES’S LAW

Charles’s Law: Temperature & Volume

Jacques Charles studied the direct mathematical relationship between temperature and volume of a gas.
Charles measured the volume of air at different temperatures and recorded the results.
As temperature increases, the volume of the gas increases. (P and n constant)

CHARLES’S LAW

Charles’s Mathematical Law

Volume ÷ temperature stays constant when pressure and amount of gas don’t change.
CHARLES’S LAW
V1T1 = V2T2
V1, T1starting volume and temperature
V2, T2new volume and temperature
P, nheld constant
⚠️ Temperature must be expressed in Kelvin.
CHARLES’S LAW

Example: Charles’s Law

A gas has a volume of 3.0 L at 400 K. What is its volume at 500 K?
STEP 1 · LIST THE VARIABLES
V1 = 3.0 L · T1 = 400 K · T2 = 500 K · V2 = ?

Charles’s law: V1T1 = V2T2
STEP 3 · PLUG IN & CALCULATE
V2 = V1T2T1 = (3.0 L)(500 K)400 K = 3.8 L
GAY-LUSSAC’S LAW

Gay-Lussac’s Law: Temperature & Pressure

Joseph Gay-Lussac found that a direct proportion exists between Kelvin temperature and pressure.
As a gas is heated, the kinetic energy of the particles increases, increasing both the frequency and energy of the collisions.
When volume remains constant, as temperature increases the pressure of the gas increases. (V and n constant)

GAY-LUSSAC’S LAW

Example: Gay-Lussac’s Law 🚗

A car tire has a pressure of 2.0 atm at 27°C. After a long drive the tire is at 57°C. What is the new pressure? (Volume is constant.)

Gay-Lussac’s law: P1T1 = P2T2

COMBINED GAS LAW

Combined Gas Law

Boyle’s, Charles’s, and Gay-Lussac’s laws combine into a single law for a fixed amount of gas.
COMBINED GAS LAW
P1V1T1 = P2V2T2
P, V, Tall three can change at once
nfixed amount of gas
Ktemperature in Kelvin
🎈 Cover the variable that stays constant and you get Boyle’s, Charles’s or Gay-Lussac’s law back.
COMBINED GAS LAW

Example: Combined Gas Law 🎈

A gas occupies 2.00 L at 1.00 atm and 300 K. What is its volume at 2.50 atm and 400 K?
STEP 1 · LIST THE VARIABLES
P1 = 1.00 atm · V1 = 2.00 L · T1 = 300 K · P2 = 2.50 atm · T2 = 400 K
STEP 2 · CHOOSE THE LAW
Combined: P1V1T1 = P2V2T2
STEP 3 · PLUG IN & CALCULATE
V2 = P1V1T2P2T1 = (1.00 atm)(2.00 L)(400 K)(2.50 atm)(300 K) = 1.07 L

AVOGADRO & DALTON

Avogadro’s Principle

Equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain an equal number of particles.
So a gas with a larger volume must consist of a greater number of particles.
At constant T and P, the number of moles is directly proportional to the volume: V1n1 = V2n2
AVOGADRO & DALTON

Dalton’s Law of Partial Pressures

The sum of the partial pressures of all the components in a gas mixture is equal to the total pressure of the gas mixture.
PT = Pa + Pb + Pc + …
EXAMPLE: AIR AT SEA LEVEL 🌍
IDEAL GAS LAW
Ideal Gas Law
Avogadro’s principle and the laws of Boyle, Charles, and Gay-Lussac combine into a single mathematical statement relating pressure, volume, temperature, and number of moles of a gas.
This law works best for gases that obey the assumptions of the kinetic-molecular theory, known as ideal gases.
Real gases act almost ideally at ordinary temperatures and pressures.
PV=nRT
THE IDEAL GAS LAW
one law for P, V, n and T

Example: Ideal Gas Law

How many moles of gas are in a 5.00 L tank at 2.00 atm and 27°C?
STEP 1 · LIST THE VARIABLES
P = 2.00 atm · V = 5.00 L · T = 300 K · R = 0.0821 · n = ?

Ideal gas law: PV = nRT
STEP 3 · PLUG IN & CALCULATE
n = PVRT = (2.00 atm)(5.00 L)(0.0821 L·atmmol·K)(300 K) = 0.406 mol
IDEAL GAS LAW

Molar Mass & the Ideal Gas Law

To find the molar mass of a gas sample, the mass, temperature, pressure and volume of the gas must be known.
PV = nRT
substitute n = mM
PV = mRTM
M = mRTPV
Mmolar mass (g/mol)
mmass of the sample (g)
EXAMPLE1.00 g of gas fills 0.700 L at 1.00 atm and 273 K:M = (1.00)(0.0821)(273)(1.00)(0.700) = 32.0 g/mol
IDEAL GAS LAW

Density & the Ideal Gas Law

To find the density of a gas sample, the temperature, pressure and molar mass of the gas must be known.
M = mRTPV
substitute mV = D
M = DRTP
D = MPRT
Ddensity (g/L)
🎈Hotter gas (higher T) lower density — hot air rises!
EXAMPLECO2 (44.0 g/mol) at 1.00 atm and 273 K:D = (44.0)(1.00)(0.0821)(273) = 1.96 g/L
