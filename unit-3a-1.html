LIGHT & WAVES

All Waves Have 4 Characteristics

1 · Amplitude
The height of the wave, measured from the origin to its crest (peak).
The brightness, or intensity, of light depends on amplitude. 💡
2 · Wavelengthλ
The distance between equivalent points on a wave.
Usually measured crest to crest or trough to trough.
3 · Frequencyν
How fast the wave oscillates up and down — the number of waves that pass a point per second.
Units: Hertz (Hz) or cycles per second (1/s, s⁻¹)
4 · Speedc
Regardless of its wavelength, light moves through space at a constant speed.
c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s
LIGHT & WAVES

The Frequency–Wavelength Relationship

Because light moves at a constant speed, there is a relationship between frequency and wavelength.
c = λν
c — speed of light (3.00 × 10⁸ m/s)
λ — wavelength (m, nm, etc.)
ν — frequency (Hz, 1/s)
Shorter wavelength
→ higher frequency
Longer wavelength
→ lower frequency

LIGHT & WAVES · EXAMPLE

Find the frequency of a photon with a wavelength of 434 nm.

GIVEN
ν = ?

c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s
WORK · click to reveal
ν = c ÷ λ
ν = (3.00 × 10⁸ m/s) ÷ (4.34 × 10⁻⁷ m)
ν = 6.91 × 10¹⁴ Hz

QUANTUM THEORY
Planck's Quantum Theory
In 1900, German physicist Max Planck hypothesized that energy, instead of being given off in continuous waves, is given off in little packets of energy, or quanta.
The word quantum means a fixed amount — think of it as flashes of energy. ⚡
⚡
QUANTUM = FIXED AMOUNT
1900 · Max Planck
QUANTUM THEORY

Planck's Equation

One quantum of energy (light) is related to its frequency by:
E = hν
Eenergy (J)
hPlanck's constant
6.6262 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s
νfrequency of the radiation (Hz)
QUANTUM THEORY

Energy Comes in Steps 🪜

🚗 What we expect: continuous
When you accelerate in a car, your speed increases from 0 to 60 mph continuously — every speed in between.
⚛️ Planck: discrete levels
At the atomic level, you press the accelerator and…
10 mphenough energy absorbed — leap!
20 mphmore energy absorbed — leap!
30 mph…and so on
Why don't we notice? Each quantum is so small (h ≈ 10⁻³⁴) that it seems continuous — like a computer-drawn line that looks smooth until you zoom in and see the blocks.
QUANTUM THEORY

The Photoelectric Effect

Albert Einstein used Planck's equation to explain his work on the photoelectric effect.
1
Light shines on a metal
›
2
Electrons are ejected from its surface
›
3
An electric current is created
But not every kind of light would eject the electron from the metal… 🤔
QUANTUM THEORY

Why Only Some Light Works

Einstein realized the energy (and thus the frequency) of the light determines whether it can knock an e⁻ from the atom. For each metal, a minimum frequency of light is needed to release electrons.
Red light ❌
Low frequency → low energy. Cannot produce a current.
Violet light ✅
High frequency → high energy. Can produce a current.

ATOMIC MODELS
Line Spectra
When elements are placed in a flame, they produce different colors of light. 🔥
Through a prism, this light doesn't make a continuous spectrum — it splits into individual lines of only certain colors, called a line spectrum.
Each element has its own unique atomic emission spectrum — it can be used to identify the element.

ATOMIC MODELS

The Bohr Model

Why specific lines of color instead of all colors? In 1911, Danish physicist Niels Bohr put Rutherford's atomic model and Planck's quantum theory together to begin to explain line spectra.
To get spectral lines, the energy of the electron must be quantized.
The electron may only have certain orbits, corresponding to different amounts of energy.
Each energy level (orbit) is labeled by a quantum number, n.

ATOMIC MODELS

The Quantum Mechanical Model of the Atom

Louis de Broglie1924
A French physics graduate student who proposed that all moving particles, including electrons, travel as waves — not in straight-line circular orbits as Bohr suggested.
λ = h ⁄ mν
Erwin Schrödinger1926
An Austrian physicist who furthered de Broglie's wave–particle theory.
He derived an equation for the electron's "wave function" — the probability of finding the electron within a particular volume of space around the nucleus.
ATOMIC MODELS

Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle

"It is fundamentally impossible to know precisely both the velocity and position of a particle at the same time."
— Werner Heisenberg
You can find out where the electron is, but not where it's going — or where it's going, but not where it is!
To detect an electron you might use a photon — but the collision moves the electron, so the best you can tell is where it was.
